Knowing,”28 or Kripke’s causal theory of reference.29 This is part of a tradition
of using causation in the explanation of other phenomena, which has become
quite respectable in philosophy. Those opposed to causal decision theory either
belong to a now rather unfashionable camp of thinking that causation is so
problematic it is not to be used in explaining anything else (Jeffrey might have
belonged to that camp; so does Arif Ahmed30; or they have a theory of
causation which is based on a theory of decision and action (Huw Price31 comes
to mind). I take causation to be a very basic concept, not reducible to anything
else (see Edgington32). So it is quite proper to appeal to it in a theory of decision.
Lewis33 is open to the possibility that decision theory needs to be up-front
about causation. But Lewis has only two kinds of conditional available to him:
the truth–functional conditional and his counterfactual conditional. The probability of the truth–functional conditional is a nonstarter for feeding into a
theory of decision. I have argued that the probability of a Lewisian counterfactual doesn’t work either. What we need instead are conditional probabilities,
of a causal sort.
