Egan’s examples exploit a very general problem for the claim that what
matters in decision taking is the probability of a conditional proposition. No
two contingent, logically independent propositions are probabilistically independent in all probability distributions—or in all
belief distributions. There are always possible states of information in which
one bears positively or negatively on the other. Maybe conditional propositions are normally independent of their antecedents, in which case it doesn’t
matter whether you consider p(A→C) or p((A→C)|A). But where they do
come apart, as in Egan’s examples, it is the conditional probability that
matters: the probability of the conditional on the assumption that you do the
action. Also, on (almost) any understanding of the conditional proposition,
p((A→C)|A) = p(C|A).18 So: (provided we are focusing on the restricted
category based on causal relations) it is sometimes essential to use conditional probabilities, and it never does any harm to use the conditional
probabilities.
