The following theorem gives us a useful tool for working with inverses.
Theorem 9.5. Let A and B be sets. Let iA and iB be the identity functions on A and B, respectively.
Suppose f : A → B, g : B → A, and g ◦ f = iA and f ◦ g = iB . Then g = f −1 .
Proof. This proof is a nice reminder that functions are sets of ordered pairs.
Let A and B be sets, f : A → B, g : B → A, and g ◦ f = iA and f ◦ g = iB .
Suppose (b, a) ∈ g (i.e., g(b) = a).
Then f (g(b)) = iB (b) = b, i.e., f (a) = b. So (a, b) ∈ f and thus (b, a) ∈ f −1 .
Hence, (b, a) ∈ g implies (b, a) ∈ f −1 .
Thus, g ⊆ f −1 .
Now, suppose (b, a) ∈ f −1 .
Then (a, b) ∈ f , so f (a) = b.
Then g(b) = g(f (a)) = iA (a) = a. Hence, (b, a) ∈ g.
Hence, (b, a) ∈ f −1 implies (b, a) ∈ g.
Thus, f −1 ⊆ g.
Since g ⊆ f −1 and f −1 ⊆ g, we conclude that g = f −1 .
