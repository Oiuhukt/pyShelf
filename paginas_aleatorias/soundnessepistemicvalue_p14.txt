IV. Taking stock
This article focused on the purported epistemic value of soundness arguments,
and, in particular, it investigated two central epistemological aspects of such
arguments: The question of whether soundness arguments can be employed
to justify or overcome reasonable open-mindedness about their conclusion.
It has been argued that both questions should receive a negative answer.

First, we can see that, according to the proposed analysis, the “entangled” version of the soundness argument [SOUND] presented an immediate case of failure: The consistency of our theory of truth CT[S] gave us the consistency of
the target theory S directly simply by the fact that S was contained in CT[S].
On the other hand, [SOUND ] presents a less immediate case of failure: The
consistency of CT[S] directly gives us the consistency of S* only. In order
to conclude that PA must be consistent as well, we needed the additional information that S* and PA are equiconsistent so that the consistency of S*
(and of CT[S]) is incompatible with PA being inconsistent.28 The additional
step involving the notion of equiconsistency is crucial: It gives us a way of
generalizing the analysis to the target cases, where the syntactic containment
assumption is lifted. Finally, it is essential to point out that the equiconsistency between S* and PA is not some ‘external fact’ that only we can appreciate:
It is provable in CT[S]. This is important because, for this strategy against
transmission and cogency to be in good standing, we want the information
about the equiconsistency to be accessible—upon reflection—to the agent accepting CT[S], and this is ensured by its provability in CT[S]. With this,
we can naturally generalize our original diagnosis to soundness arguments
in general. Where does this conclusion leave us? The remainder of this article makes some important yet exploratory remarks about my analysis’s scope,
consequences, and ramifications.
